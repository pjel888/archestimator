# Real-World Incident — jigs

**Research status:** `DESK_AUDIT_COMPLETE / MARKET_VALIDATION_PENDING`

A design team was working on a 13 m × 6 m pool bar in Palawan. The original structural design used concrete columns and steel beams. Because the team wanted to avoid placing a column in the middle of the bar, the steel beams had to span farther and required a depth of roughly 300 mm.

That beam size was not readily available from suppliers near the project site. The client suggested using lightweight or light-gauge steel instead through a supplier they personally knew.

The design team met with the supplier over Zoom and discussed the structural situation and project schedule. The supplier committed to completing the structural computations and production within two weeks. The lightweight channels could also be transported to Palawan and assembled on site, making the proposal appear faster and easier to deliver than the original steel-beam solution.

The members were fabricated, delivered, and installed. After installation, however, the steel member deflected near the middle and caused part of the roof to sag. The roof had only about a 2% slope because of existing structural constraints, so the sag disrupted water runoff and contributed to leaking.

Water seeped through the roofing and damaged most of the 3/4-inch marine-plywood substrate underneath. The supplier attempted to reinforce the deflected member by adding tubular steel across it, but visible deflection remained.

The problem is still being resolved. The likely remedial work includes removing the roofing, replacing the damaged plywood substrate, and installing new roofing. The team associates the failure with the lightweight-steel substitution, although the exact technical cause has not yet been conclusively established.

For Archestimator, this is a concrete example of why a material or structural substitution should preserve the original design, reason for change, supplier proposal, calculations, assumptions, schedule promise, approvals, unresolved risks, installed evidence, and resulting remedial work. Archestimator could preserve what was proposed and accepted; it would not certify whether the engineering solution was structurally adequate.
